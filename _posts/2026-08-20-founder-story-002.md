I usually don't start with a product.

I start with questions.

How much does it cost?

Can this be built for free?

Which local model can actually run?

How do we connect the models?

How do we make one hundred models work together?

How do we train the model we already have?

How do we get the first customer?

How do we sell it?

How do we make it survive?

These are not separate questions for me.

They become one system.

## Before starting

One of my notebook pages literally begins with:

> "Questions Before Starting"

and then goes straight into the economics and mechanics of becoming fully solopreneur from sub-zero: app cost, domains, development without paid tooling, local reasoning, deployment, model training, customer acquisition, income, and scale.

The order matters.

Before building the thing, I want to know what the thing depends on.

Before paying for a dependency, I want to know whether I can replace it.

Before hiring, I want to know whether software can remove the work.

Before building one product, I want to know whether the underlying capability can be reused.

That is how the questions start connecting.

## The page becomes a system

A notebook page can look messy.

Arrows. Numbers. Half-sentences. A model name here. A price there. A question about customers on the next line.

But the mess is useful.

It is the raw architecture.

One page asks about local reasoning. Another connects phone data to a laptop. Another asks how a voice system should listen, understand, and react. Another moves from data collection to model training and then to a product.

The page is not the final design.

It is where the design starts appearing.

## Why I keep the questions

A finished product hides the uncertainty that existed before it.

The questions preserve it.

That is useful because the next product usually starts with a question from the previous product.

The system grows by carrying the unanswered questions forward.

## What this newspaper will preserve

I don't want to publish only the clean version.

The newspaper will keep three layers visible:

**RAW** — the original question, sketch, or thought.

**SYSTEM** — what the idea became when the pieces were connected.

**STORY** — what changed after building it.

That makes the publication more than a newsletter.

It becomes a record of how a system was discovered.

And that is closer to how I actually work.
